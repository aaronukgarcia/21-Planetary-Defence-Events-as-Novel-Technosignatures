# Planetary Defence Events as Technosignatures

*What would be detectable, and what would not — v3.0, August 2026*

## The idea

Every civilisation that lives on a planet will eventually be in the path of something large. Over ten thousand years a Tunguska-class impact is near-certain; over longer windows, worse. Survival requires either moving the object or moving the population — and neither is quiet. That makes defensive activity a more universal expectation than radio transmission or megastructures, because being hit is not a cultural choice.

So: could we see another civilisation saving itself?

## The honest answer

**Mostly not — and not the way the idea first suggests.**

- **The optical flash is a dead end.** An earlier draft claimed moon-scale deflection events would reach naked-eye brightness at 100 light-years. That is wrong by roughly three orders of magnitude: it would require about 5,000 solar luminosities, some 3,000× the total energy of the Moon-forming impact. The arithmetic is shown in full in Section 2 so anyone can check it.
- **The infrared debris signature is real — and already detected.** Collision energy is radiated by warm dust over years, not hours. Giant impacts around other stars have been observed this way: HD 172555's vapour-condensed silica, BD +20 307's 420 K dust within 1 AU.
- **So the question changes.** We don't need a new way to detect planetary-scale events. We need a way to tell a *managed* event from a natural one. The paper proposes four discriminants — isotopic anomaly, statistical orbital clearing, repetition on orbital timescales, and evacuation thermal signatures — and states honestly that only one (repetition) is within reach of current instruments.

## Corrections in this version

v2 was titled "revised following peer review" and thanked reviewers, but had not acted on the three physics errors a review had identified. All three are corrected here and described openly:

1. The optical magnitude estimate (above).
2. **Technetium-99 is produced naturally** — by slow neutron capture in AGB stars; Merrill's 1952 detection was the first proof of stellar nucleosynthesis. The technosignature argument survives only in qualified form: Tc-99 is anomalous in a *cold main-sequence* system because it should have decayed and cannot be replenished there.
3. **The tritium supply-chain constraint was wrong** — staged thermonuclear devices breed tritium in situ from lithium-6 deuteride.

No external peer review has been sought or obtained for this work, and no reviewers, advisors, or institutions have been consulted.

## What would help

Quantitative modelling of a deflection campaign's debris signature against the natural giant-impact baseline — does the difference survive at interstellar distance? If it doesn't, that's worth publishing too. Criticism of the arithmetic in Sections 2 and 3 is especially welcome; it's shown in full for that purpose.
